How to hire an SEO expert: a step-by-step process for 2026

By Stanislav Peev, SEO consultant & GEO specialist · Published October 28, 2026 · 13 min read

To hire an SEO expert well, decide which problem you're hiring for, write a one-page brief, put the same questions to three candidates in writing, start with a paid audit or a small trial, and only then sign an ongoing contract. This guide walks through each step, with Google's own advice on hiring an SEO and what the search results for "hire SEO expert" actually put in front of you.

Key takeaways

  • Hire for a problem. "SEO expert", "specialist", "consultant" and "freelancer" are used loosely; what matters is whether you need diagnosis or execution
  • 5 of the top 10 US Google results for "hire seo consultant" on October 28, 2026 were marketplaces and talent networks. Platforms rank well; they don't vet for your niche
  • Read-only first. Google's own guidance says to grant only read access to Search Console while an SEO audits your site
  • No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google - Google says so in its hiring guide. A guarantee is a reason to walk away
  • Ask about AI search. Google now lists "optimizing for generative AI" among legitimate SEO services, and asks you to check that any GEO advice matches its official guidance

1. Do you need to hire an SEO expert?

Not always. Google's own guidance says that if you run a small local business, you can probably do much of the work yourself, starting with its SEO starter guide.

Google also names the best moment to bring someone in: before a site redesign or a new site launch, so the site is built search-friendly from the start. From my side of the table, hiring pays off in five situations:

  • Traffic stalled or dropped and you don't know why.
  • A redesign or migration is coming - the cheapest time to catch SEO problems is before they ship.
  • Your time costs more than the fee. Business owners who tried both estimate that doing SEO themselves takes 3-10 times longer (from my guide on what an SEO professional does).
  • An agency is pitching you a retainer and you want an independent second opinion first.
  • AI assistants describe your business wrongly and you don't know which sources to fix.

If none of these apply, do the free basics first. My small business checklist covers them.

2. Consultant, freelancer, agency or employee?

The titles overlap, so decide by the work. Diagnosis and direction need seniority; execution needs hours. Most bad hires come from buying one when you needed the other.

WhoWhat you typically getBest for
Independent SEO consultantSenior diagnosis, strategy and oversight, often with the technical work done personallyAudits, direction, second opinions, teams that can implement
Freelance SEO specialistHands-on execution of defined tasks: on-page fixes, technical tickets, content updatesClear, scoped work with someone else setting priorities
SEO agencyA team covering strategy, content, links and technical work at volumeLarger budgets and no in-house marketing team
In-house SEO specialistA full-time employee who owns searchBusinesses where search is a core, permanent channel

One practical note on wording. In US search, "SEO specialist" is mostly a job-title query: on October 28, 2026 the top Google results for it were Indeed, career guides and salary pages. If you want outside help rather than an employee, search for an SEO consultant or agency instead. The roles also combine well: a consultant sets priorities, and a freelancer or your own team executes them.

Each channel has a different trade-off:

  • Open marketplaces (Upwork, Freelancer). You post a job and receive proposals. The range is wide, from excellent to dangerous, and the vetting is entirely yours.
  • Vetted networks and tiers (Toptal, Fiverr Pro). The platform screens first. Toptal says it accepts the top 3% of applicants and offers a trial period where you pay only if satisfied; Fiverr Pro labels its sellers as vetted by the Fiverr Pro team. These are the platforms' own claims, so use them as a first filter and check fit for your market yourself.
  • Independent consultants. Found through referrals, published work, conference talks and LinkedIn. Look for public work that shows how the person thinks: case studies with dates and numbers, research, teardowns. Someone who publishes their method is easier to vet than someone who only publishes testimonials.
  • Agencies. Usually found through directories and "best SEO agencies" lists. Check who published the list: many are paid placements or written by an agency that ranks itself first. I looked at how AI treats those lists in a separate guide.
  • Communities. Reddit and industry forums are good for references and honest warnings. A stranger who sends you a private message offering SEO services is cold outreach, wherever it arrives.

And one channel to skip: Google tells site owners to be wary of SEO firms, consultants or agencies that email out of the blue.

4. Write a one-page brief

A short written brief makes every candidate answer the same question, and it shows you within one reply who actually read it.

Put these on one page:

  • What you sell and to whom - your best customers, even if you could serve more.
  • Markets and languages - countries, cities, and whether you need more than one language.
  • The business goal - leads, calls, sales or bookings - and how you measure it today.
  • What changed - a traffic drop, a redesign, a new market, a new competitor.
  • What you can give - access to Search Console and analytics, a developer, a writer, internal time.
  • Budget range and timeline - a range is enough; it saves both sides a week of guessing.
  • What you've already tried - and what happened.
  • The AI question - what ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity say about you when asked for your category. Paste one answer if you have it.

Then watch the replies. Google's guidance says a good SEO should be interested in your business: what makes it unique, who your competitors are, how your customers find you. A reply that skips your brief and sends a package list is your first filter.

5. Questions to ask an SEO expert before you hire

Ask every candidate the same questions in writing. The first five come from Google's own hiring guide; the last three are mine, and they separate senior people from sellers quickly.

6. Start with a paid audit or a small trial

Before an ongoing contract, buy one small, fixed piece of work. It's the cheapest way to see how someone thinks.

For a consultant, that's usually a fixed-scope audit. For a freelancer, a small, well-defined task. Some platforms build this in; Toptal, for example, describes a trial period you pay for only if satisfied. Whatever the format, Google's guidance gives three rules for the audit stage:

  • Grant read access only. Search Console read access is enough for an audit; Google says not to grant write access at this stage.
  • Expect realistic estimates. An audit should give you realistic estimates of improvement and of the work involved. If it promises first place in the results, Google's advice is to find someone else.
  • Check the recommendations. Before you make big changes based on a tool's audit, check them against Google's official documentation. A good SEO cites it without being asked.

Judge the result on two things: did the audit tell you something you didn't know about your own business, and does every recommendation come with a reason? If it reads like a crawler export with a logo on it, you've learned what the retainer would look like.

7. Contract, access and ownership

Whatever you sign, keep the keys. Google is blunt about why: you are ultimately responsible for the actions of any company you hire.

  • You own every account - Search Console, analytics, Google Business Profile, the domain, hosting. Add the SEO as a user; don't share passwords.
  • Every change is logged. Google says that an SEO with server access should be willing to explain all the changes they make to your site.
  • You never have to link to them. Google says it plainly: you should never have to link to an SEO. No mandatory footer credits.
  • Reporting on outcomes. Monthly, in business terms first: leads, calls, sales. Rankings and traffic as supporting detail.
  • A short initial term. A month-to-month arrangement or a short first term with a clear notice period, so you stay only if results justify it.
  • What happens at the end. Content, data and any accounts created for you stay yours.

8. Red flags

Google's hiring guide lists most of them itself. If you see one, the conversation is over.

  • Guaranteed rankings. Google: no one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google.
  • A "special relationship with Google" or "priority submit". Google names both as warning signs, and it never accepts money to include or rank sites in organic results.
  • Secrecy. A company that won't clearly explain what it intends to do.
  • Unsolicited emails offering to fix your rankings.
  • Link schemes or "submission to thousands of search engines". Google calls these typically useless exercises.
  • Deceptive content made on your behalf - Google warns that it can get a site removed from its index entirely.

For the warnings US business owners give each other after bad hires, see the red-flag section of my guide on what a real SEO professional does.

9. Hiring for AI search (GEO) in 2026

The job now includes AI answers, and Google's hiring guide says so: "optimizing for generative AI" sits on its list of services that SEOs legitimately provide.

The same page adds a check: if a provider advises on AI experiences, what it calls "AEO" or "GEO", is that advice aligned with Google's official guidance on generative AI features? Three more questions are worth asking:

  • How do you measure AI visibility? Answers vary from run to run and between engines. In my 60-query experiment, the choice of engine moved a brand's measured visibility about 2.3 times more than the model's own randomness. A single blended "AI score" hides that.
  • Do you separate mentions from citations? Being cited as a source and being recommended are different outcomes; good reports show both.
  • Where would you fix a wrong fact? The right answer is at its source - the page the model read - not in a prompt or a chatbot setting.

Be careful with two popular offers. Self-ranked "best of" lists and owned review sites get cited, but in one 100-query test AI Overviews cited such lists 323 times and still didn't recommend their author in 69% of cases (source-checked here). And Reddit posting packages sold for ChatGPT visibility rest on a share that moves fast: Reddit's share of ChatGPT Search citations fell 86% in August 2026 in one tracker's data (details here). 10. What the first 90 days should show

By day 90 you should see shipped changes and early movement on top of the reports. Competitive rankings take longer, but activity and leading indicators should not.

  • Month 1: access set up, the audit or diagnosis delivered, a baseline recorded (Search Console, analytics, and what AI assistants say about you), and a prioritized plan you understand.
  • Month 2: the first fixes live, with a change log you can read.
  • Month 3: movement in leading indicators: pages indexed, impressions, positions on the target pages, corrected facts in AI answers. Competitive rankings and steady lead flow typically take 4-12 months.

If at day 90 you have polished reports but nothing shipped, that's the signal to renegotiate or move on.

11. Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to hire an SEO expert?

It depends on the model (audit, hourly or monthly consulting, full-service retainer), seniority and how competitive your market is, so an average tells you little. Scope first: a fixed-price audit shows what's worth doing and what result is realistic before you commit to ongoing spend.

Should I hire an SEO freelancer, a consultant or an agency?

Hire for the problem. A senior consultant for diagnosis, strategy and oversight; a freelancer for scoped execution; an agency for volume on a larger budget. Consultant-led strategy with freelance or in-house execution is a common mix.

Can I hire an SEO expert on Upwork or Fiverr?

Yes. On open marketplaces the vetting is yours; vetted tiers like Fiverr Pro and networks like Toptal screen first by their own criteria. Run the same process either way: brief, identical questions, a small paid trial.

What questions should I ask an SEO expert before hiring?

Previous work, Google Search Essentials, expected results and timeframe, experience in your industry and country, and whether every change is shared and explained. Then: what they do personally, when they'd advise against SEO, and what AI assistants say about you now. The table above shows good and bad answers.

What access should I give an SEO expert?

Read-only Search Console and analytics during an audit; Google says not to grant write access at that stage. Later, add them as a user on accounts you own, never share passwords, and ask for a change log.

How long does it take to see results?

Technical fixes can move numbers within weeks; competitive rankings and steady lead flow typically take 4-12 months. By day 90 you should see shipped changes and movement in leading indicators.
