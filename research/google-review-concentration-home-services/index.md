# Google review concentration: who really owns the reviews in a local market

By **Stanislav Peev**, SEO consultant & GEO specialist · Published **August 24, 2026** · Original dataset, 7,072 listings · 9 min read

5.6%

Does having the most Google reviews get you into the Maps top 3? Almost never. Across 72 US home services markets, the most-reviewed company held a top-3 position in just 5.6% of them.

Original analysis of 7,072 Google Business Profile listings, collected August 24, 2026. Method and limitations.

## Key findings

- The review leader held a top-3 Maps position in **5.6%** of markets. Its median position was **#35**.
- The median first-ranked listing had **121 reviews**. The median review leader had **4,447** - and usually lost to it.
- Review count barely tracks position: median Spearman correlation **0.164**, with 11 of 72 markets running **negative**.
- Reviews are still heavily concentrated: the top 5 listings hold a median **58.3%** of the top 20's review volume.
- Trade decides difficulty, not city size. Top-5 share runs **48.0%** in roofing versus **73.8%** in electrical.
- Metro size barely moves it: **60.2%** in large metros, **58.9%** in small ones.
- The top-3 ranked listings carried only **1.96x** the market median - a threshold to clear, not a race to win.

Every guide on local search tells owners the same thing: collect more Google reviews and you will climb the map pack. The advice is everywhere, and the benchmark articles behind it are real research. But they all answer one question - how many reviews does a business in your category typically have - and then stop.

Nobody publishes the other half: how those reviews are *distributed* inside a single market. A benchmark tells you whether you are above average. It does not tell you whether the market is still winnable. The question is live: a business owner recently [pulled data on 136 contractors across three cities](https://www.reddit.com/r/smallbusiness/comments/1vqy0f1/does_anyone_elses_trade_have_this_big_a_gap/), found the top 5 holding 69% of all reviews, asked whether that pattern generalises - and got no sourced answer. So I collected the data.

This is a companion piece to my earlier experiment on [how reliable AI visibility tracking actually is](https://stanislav-peev.com/research/ai-visibility-tracking-reliability/). Both started the same way - a number that everyone quotes, and nobody has measured.

## Does the most-reviewed company rank first on Google Maps?

No. Across 72 markets the company with the most reviews held a top-3 Maps position only 5.6% of the time, and its median position was #35. The median first-ranked listing had 121 reviews while the median review leader had 4,447.

In 19.4% of markets the review leader did not even make the top 10. The rank correlation confirms it: the median Spearman coefficient between review count and position was 0.164, which is close to no relationship at all. In 44 of 72 markets the correlation sat below 0.2 in absolute terms, and in 11 markets it was outright negative - more reviews, worse position.

Phoenix roofing is a clean illustration. The listing in position one had 44 reviews. The company in position nine had 1,053. Both were visible; the smaller profile was more visible.

**What this does not say.** This measures the ordering of the result set Google returns for a city-level query. It does not control for proximity to the search centroid, which is a major ranking input, so this is not a claim that reviews do not matter. Whitespark's ranking factors work puts reviews at roughly [17% of map pack weight](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-reviews-impact-rankings/429783/), and that is consistent with what I found: meaningful, but one input among many. The narrower claim the data supports is that review volume on its own does not buy visible position, and the businesses winning these result sets are usually not the ones with the biggest review counts.

## How concentrated are Google reviews in a local trade?

Heavily. The five most-reviewed listings in a market hold a median 58.3% of the review volume of the top 20. The top three hold 44.9%, and the single leader alone holds 21.2%.

The gap between the leader and a typical competitor is the part that surprises people. Across all 72 markets, the most-reviewed listing had a median of 107.1 times the reviews of the median listing in the same market. Meanwhile the median listing itself sat at just 40 reviews, and about 5% of returned listings had none at all.

So both things are true at once: a handful of firms own most of the review volume, and the typical competitor you are actually up against has fewer than fifty reviews. Concentration at the top does not mean the field is strong.

The spread is wide enough to change strategy. In roofing, the leader has about 30 times the median listing and the top five hold under half the volume - a market where a disciplined review programme can realistically move you into the visible set. In electrical the leader has over 600 times the median. No review programme closes that gap, which means the route in has to be something other than out-reviewing the leader.

## Are smaller cities easier to compete in?

Not on this measure. Median top-5 share was 60.2% in large metros, 57.8% in mid-sized ones and 58.9% in small ones. The differences are inside the noise.

This one contradicts standard advice directly. "Go to a smaller market, it is less competitive" is repeated constantly, and on review concentration it is simply not true - Boise electrical is more concentrated than Chicago roofing by a wide margin. Absolute review counts are lower in small metros, so the numbers you need to hit are smaller, but the shape of the market is the same. If you are choosing where to expand, the trade you pick matters far more than the city.

## Why electricians look more locked up than they are

The leaders in electrician results are usually not electricians. They are multi-trade home services conglomerates that also appear in plumbing and HVAC searches, and they drag the concentration figure up with them.

I found 41 companies with 500 or more reviews appearing in three or more different trade searches in the same city. The clearest case is Parker & Sons in Phoenix: one profile with 34,733 reviews that is simultaneously the review leader for HVAC, plumbing and electrical.

The category looks brutally concentrated because a handful of plumbing and HVAC groups with five-figure review counts appear in the same result set. If you are an electrician reading your category benchmark and despairing, the benchmark is describing a different business model to yours.

## How many Google reviews do you actually need?

Roughly double your local median. The top-3 ranked listings carried a median of 1.96x the market median, against a leader-to-median ratio of 107.1x. Reviews behave like a threshold to clear, not a race to win.

That single ratio is the most actionable number in the dataset. The businesses that actually hold visible positions are not the ones with the most reviews - they are the ones comfortably above the local middle. Going from 20 reviews to 80 in a market with a median of 40 changes your standing. Going from 800 to 3,000 appears to buy very little.

The practical move is to stop benchmarking against the category leader and start benchmarking against the median of the listings you actually compete with. That number is usually far smaller and far more reachable than the figure in a national benchmark report. It is the same logic I apply in a [technical SEO audit](https://stanislav-peev.com/services/seo-audit/): measure the field you are in, not the field the industry writes about.

## Why published review benchmarks disagree by 40x

Because they sample different things in different years. Published figures for reaching the local top 3 range from 47 reviews to 2,084, and no source reconciles them.

The [BrightLocal Google Reviews Study](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/google-reviews-study/) covers 93,845 businesses and reports an average of 39 reviews, with top-3 businesses averaging 47 - but its data is from 2018. [Local Falcon's whitepaper](https://www.localfalcon.com/blog/whitepaper-google-reviews-ratings--location-what-50-million-search-results-reveal-about-ranking-in-the-local-3pack), built on 50 million search results, reports category medians of 215 for plumbers and a 90th percentile above 2,000 for HVAC. Both are careful pieces of work. They are measuring different populations, at different times, with different definitions of "ranking".

My own figure - a median of 121 reviews for the first-ranked listing - sits between them, which is what you would expect from a third sampling frame. The lesson for an owner is not that one study is wrong. It is that a national benchmark cannot tell you what your market requires, because the variance between markets is larger than the variance between studies.

## Methodology and limitations

Data was collected on August 24, 2026 through the DataForSEO Google Maps endpoint at depth 100, covering six home service trades across twelve US cities chosen to span three metro-size bands. All 72 queries returned successfully.

- **Sample:** 7,072 listings, 2,215,490 reviews, 72 trade-and-city markets
- **Trades:** roofing, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, landscaping, garage door repair
- **Cities:** Phoenix, Atlanta, Dallas, Chicago, Denver, Nashville, Portland, Kansas City, Boise, Des Moines, Chattanooga, Fort Wayne
- **Concentration denominator:** the top 20 most-reviewed listings, held fixed so that markets returning fewer than 100 listings stay comparable. Full-set figures are in the published data
- **Collected:** August 24, 2026 · **Update schedule:** annually on the same grid

**Limitations, stated plainly.** The visible result set is not a census of every registered business in a city, so concentration is measured within the competitive set Google actually returns. The rank findings describe the ordering of that returned set for a city-level query and do not control for proximity to the search centroid, so they should not be read as a ranking-factor experiment. The sample is US-only, six trades, at a single point in time. Review counts include all reviews on a profile regardless of age. Four of the 72 markets returned fewer than 100 listings; the fixed top-20 denominator reduces but does not eliminate that effect.

The raw data is published so the analysis can be checked and rerun.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do you need the most reviews to rank first on Google Maps?**

No. Across 72 home services markets the most-reviewed company held a top-3 position only 5.6% of the time, and its median position was #35. The median first-ranked listing had 121 reviews while the median review leader had 4,447.

**How many Google reviews does a typical local contractor have?**

The median listing across 7,072 profiles was 40 reviews. It varies sharply by trade: plumbers ran a median of 70.5 and electricians just 13.5. Roughly 5% of listings had no reviews at all.

**Is it easier to compete in a smaller city?**

Not on review concentration. Median top-5 share was 60.2% in large metros, 57.8% in mid-sized and 58.9% in small ones. Which trade you are in matters far more than which city you are in.

**Which home services trade is hardest to break into on reviews?**

Electricians, where the top 5 hold 73.8% of the visible review volume and the leader has 634 times the reviews of the median listing. Much of that is driven by plumbing and HVAC conglomerates appearing in electrician results.

**How many reviews do I actually need to compete?**

The top-3 ranked listings carried a median of 1.96 times the market median, not hundreds of times. Reviews behave like a threshold to clear rather than a race to win, so roughly double your local median is a defensible target.

**Why do published review benchmarks disagree so much?**

Because they sample different things at different times. Figures range from 47 reviews to 2,084 depending on the study, trade and year. Benchmarks describe the leaders in a category, not the typical competitor in your city.
